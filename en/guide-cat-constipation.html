Cat Constipation: How Many Days Is Too Long?

The bottom line first: once a day is normal, and by day two without stool it is time to call the vet, not to keep watching.

Where does constipation begin? Normal frequency

Most cats pass stool about once a day. Depending on diet, portion size and age, some go twice daily and some have gone every other day their whole lives, so no single number defines normal for every cat. That is why the baseline that matters is not somebody else's figure but your own cat's usual pattern. If you scoop the box every morning, you already have the number, size and firmness of those stools memorised — and that instinct is more accurate than any reference.

Sources do differ on when "no stool" becomes "see a vet." Because once a day is normal, some advise a check-up after only one or two days without stool, while others draw the line at 48 hours. Cornell's Feline Health Center gives no specific day count but stresses that constipation should be treated fairly aggressively, because leaving it can progress to megacolon — permanent loss of colon function.

The reason the thresholds vary is simple: constipation is a condition that worsens itself over time, so wherever you draw the line, drawing it late is the riskier error. The longer stool sits in the colon, the more water the colon wall reabsorbs from it, and the harder it becomes to move. A few days of that turns a problem that plain hydration would have solved into one that needs an enema at the clinic.

Just as important as frequency is the shape of the stool. A cat that goes every day but produces only a few small, rock-hard pellets is already constipated. Conversely, a cat that goes every other day with normal thickness and firmness, and without straining, may simply be normal for that cat.

That straining may not be constipation at all

This is the part to read first. Straining in the litter box with nothing coming out happens with constipation — and it happens identically with a urethral obstruction. The urgency of the two could not be more different. In an obstruction, urine cannot leave the body and waste products and potassium build up; emergency references describe cats deteriorating rapidly beyond 24 hours, with vomiting, lethargy and an inability to stand, and the condition becoming life-threatening in that window.

Multiple sources note that owners commonly mistake an obstruction for constipation or a bladder infection, and that this misreading is exactly what delays care. Male cats are at far higher risk because their urethra is longer and narrower. Use the table below to tell them apart — but if there is any doubt at all, assume the emergency rather than the constipation.

The most reliable check is to look at the litter itself. If a normal-sized urine clump has formed after a visit to the box, urine is passing, and constipation becomes the more likely explanation. If not a single clump has formed all day, that alone is reason for emergency care. Bladder problems in general are covered separately in our guide to feline cystitis.

Why it happens — and why summer makes it worse

Constipation has many causes, but the one veterinary sources name first is dehydration. The colon's job is to recover water from stool passing through it. When the body is short of water, the colon takes back more, and what is left behind sets like stone.

Cats are poor drinkers to begin with — the usual explanation being a blunted thirst drive inherited from desert-dwelling ancestors. Summer compounds it. Panting and extra grooming increase water loss while a lukewarm, stale bowl makes a cat drink even less. You can check how much your cat should be drinking for its weight with our pet water intake calculator, and the practical ways to get more in are in how to get your cat to drink more water.

After dehydration, the most common culprit is hair. Swallowed hair binds with stool in the gut and makes it difficult to move — an issue in shedding season and, for long-haired cats, all year round. Other causes that come up repeatedly in the literature:

  • The litter box itself — a dirty box, a noisy location, or too few boxes in a multi-cat home all teach a cat to hold it. Held stool sits longer and hardens.
  • Chronic kidney disease — ongoing water loss through urine leaves the cat chronically dehydrated. It overlaps so often with constipation in older cats that our guide to feline CKD is worth reading alongside this one.
  • Past pelvic fracture — a pelvis that healed narrowed after a fall or a car accident physically shrinks the passage.
  • Nerve and muscle problems — spinal cord injury or dysautonomia can weaken the colon's ability to push.
  • Medication side effects — opioids, anticholinergics and diuretics can all slow the gut.
  • Foreign material — bone fragments, string or plastic mixed into stool can block it outright.
  • Obesity and inactivity — a sedentary cat has a slower gut.
  • Idiopathic — sometimes a full work-up finds nothing. That is called idiopathic constipation, or idiopathic megacolon.

Notice that over half of that list is invisible from home. Pelvic anatomy, kidney values and thyroid function require testing. So if constipation is not a one-off but a repeating pattern, chasing it with water alone is slower than getting the cause investigated.

Constipation → obstipation → megacolon

Veterinary medicine grades this by severity in three steps. The terms are unfamiliar, but they explain why moving early matters.

  • Constipation — infrequent or difficult passage of dry, hard stool. At this stage, hydration and diet changes plus mild treatment often resolve it.
  • Obstipation — constipation severe enough that the cat cannot pass the stool at all, with hardened masses extending from the rectum upward. This has to be cleared at a clinic.
  • Megacolon — the colon dilates and loses motility, so constipation and obstipation recur chronically. Stretched colon muscle does not readily return to normal.

In other words, megacolon is less a disease that arrives one day than the accumulated result of constipation that was never resolved. Each round of "let's give it another day" moves a cat from the reversible stage toward the stage that is not. Megacolon that fails to respond to medical management can end in a subtotal colectomy, removing much of the colon. The surgery itself has a reasonable outlook, though references note that mild to moderate diarrhoea can persist for weeks to months afterwards.

One more thing. Severe constipation can produce something that looks exactly like diarrhoea: a hard mass blocks the way and only liquid seeps past it. To an owner that reads as "she's got diarrhoea now, so the constipation must have cleared." The test is not whether something came out, but whether a normal, ordinary-sized stool came out.

What you can safely try — and what you must never do

If you are at the early-signs stage and your cat is still eating and bright, these are reasonable to try at home.

  • Shift the balance toward wet food — the single most effective change. Dry food is around 10% moisture; wet food is 70–80%. Even adding water to meals noticeably raises daily intake. Work out the daily portion with our cat food portion calculator, then adjust the wet share within it.
  • More water bowls, in better places — away from the food bowl, somewhere quiet, several of them. In summer, refresh at least twice a day.
  • Litter box count and cleanliness — one box per cat plus one is the standard advice. Scoop daily, and try a different litter if your cat dislikes the current one.
  • Brushing — reducing swallowed hair is prevention in itself. During shedding, daily is best.
  • Play for activity — two or three ten-to-fifteen minute hunting sessions a day help gut motility too.
  • Fibre comes after water — pumpkin or psyllium only makes sense once hydration is adequate. In the wrong order, you just build a bigger mass.

These, by contrast, you should never do.

  • Human enemas, especially phosphate-based ones — veterinary references state that phosphate enemas must be avoided in cats because of severe electrolyte disturbances (hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia). This can kill.
  • Human laxatives and stimulant purgatives — there is no safe feline dosing behind them.
  • Attempting an enema at home — you can injure the rectum, and with a hardened mass in place it will not work anyway.
  • Pressing or massaging the abdomen hard — painful, and potentially dangerous depending on what is going on.
  • Milk or oils on your own initiative — many cats digest lactose poorly, and forcing oil into a cat's mouth risks aspiration pneumonia.

In short, water and environment are yours; medication and enemas belong to the clinic. And all of that applies only while your cat is still eating and bright. If vomiting, refusal of food or lethargy have appeared, this is no longer a home-remedy situation.

What happens at the clinic

Knowing what to expect makes the decision easier. It usually runs in this order.

First, abdominal palpation and radiographs to see how much stool is loaded in the colon, whether the pelvis is narrowed, and whether foreign material is present. For recurring constipation, bloodwork and a thyroid level (T4) look for kidney disease or endocrine problems, with ultrasound or colonoscopy if needed.

Treatment scales with severity. Mild cases get fluids to correct dehydration plus diet and fibre adjustments. Where hardened stool is loaded, an enema using warm water, saline or lactulose is given, and if that fails, manual removal under anaesthesia. Afterwards, a stool softener such as lactulose or a prokinetic such as cisapride may be prescribed to prevent recurrence. Doses are adjusted to the individual cat, so never change them yourself.

For megacolon that no longer responds to medication, a subtotal colectomy is discussed. Where a recent pelvic fracture is the cause, surgery to widen the pelvis may be considered instead. To say it once more: the surest way never to reach this point is to go early.

Finally, everything here is a general guideline. The same "two days without stool" is read very differently depending on age, underlying disease and accompanying signs. Diagnosis and treatment must come from a veterinarian. If you also want to check which foods are off-limits, see foods dogs and cats can't eat.

My cat hasn't passed stool for two days. Can I wait a bit longer?

Calling your vet is better than waiting. Sources differ slightly on the threshold: because once a day is normal, some recommend a check-up after only one or two days without stool, while others draw the line at 48 hours. What they all agree on is that constipation gets worse the longer it is left. While stool sits in the colon, water keeps being drawn out of it, so it hardens further and becomes even harder to pass. On top of that, the reason your cat is straining may not be constipation at all but a urethral obstruction, which can become life-threatening within 24 hours. On day two, a phone call is safer than another day of watching.

Can I give my cat a human laxative or an over-the-counter enema?

No. Phosphate-containing enemas sold for humans can be fatal to cats. Veterinary references state plainly that phosphate enemas must be avoided in cats because they can cause severe electrolyte disturbances — hyperphosphataemia with secondary hypocalcaemia. Human stimulant laxatives and magnesium preparations are also unsafe because there is no established feline dosing behind them. Some drugs, such as lactulose, genuinely are used in cats, but the dose and frequency have to be set by a veterinarian. Treat water and diet as the part you handle at home, and medication and enemas as the part that belongs at the clinic.

Do pumpkin and psyllium actually help? How much should I give?

Several sources agree that added fibre can help mild constipation. The amount, however, varies enough between sources that no single number is honest, and more importantly, fibre only works alongside water. If you increase fibre while your cat is still under-hydrated, you may simply build a larger, firmer mass. The order matters: increase wet food and water intake first, then add a small amount of fibre. Note too that in advanced cases such as megacolon, adding fibre can make things worse, and low-residue diets are sometimes used instead. Which category your cat falls into is something to settle at the clinic, not at home.

I thought it was constipation, but now small amounts of liquid stool are coming out. Is it resolving?

Not necessarily. When a hard mass blocks the colon, liquid stool can seep around it. To an owner it looks like diarrhoea, while the blockage is still entirely in place. Veterinary references also describe vomiting and diarrhoea appearing in severe cases because of rectal irritation. The question to ask is not whether something came out, but whether a normal, ordinary-sized stool came out. If only small amounts of liquid keep appearing and your cat is still straining in the box, have it checked.
