Stomatitis in Cats: From Signs to Treatment

The bottom line first: stomatitis is not "severe gum disease" but a distinct, immune-driven condition — and the mainstay of treatment is extraction, not scaling.

Stomatitis is a different disease from tartar and periodontitis

Most owners arrive with "my cat's breath is terrible" and hear the word stomatitis for the first time at the clinic. The natural next thought is: "So one dental cleaning should fix it." Unfortunately that is not how this disease ends. Its full name is feline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS), and it is fundamentally different from the periodontitis caused by tartar building up around teeth.

The difference in one line: periodontitis is a problem around the teeth, while stomatitis is a problem of the oral mucosa as a whole. A cat with stomatitis has inflammation not only in the gums but in the inner cheeks, under the tongue, and especially in the tissue behind the molars where the mouth turns toward the throat — the palatoglossal folds. Lesions in that back region are called caudal stomatitis, and one source reports that roughly 85% of cases involve them. When that area hurts, the act of swallowing itself becomes painful.

How common the disease is varies noticeably between sources. Some summaries put it at 0.7-12% of the general cat population, while a review article mentions up to 26%. The gap likely comes from differences in study populations and diagnostic criteria, and it would be misleading to state one firm number. What all sources agree on is that it is not rare. The average age at presentation is reported at around 7 years.

This distinction matters because mistaking stomatitis for periodontitis leads to repeating dental cleanings while time passes — and the cat stays in pain throughout. That is why the area behind the molars has to be examined properly at the diagnostic stage.

Spotting it at home — the cat who cries and walks away from the bowl

Cats are extremely good at hiding pain. So stomatitis rarely announces itself as "my mouth hurts"; it shows up as something else first. The most commonly described scene is this: the cat runs to the bowl hungry, takes a bite or two, then gives a short cry or shakes its head and walks away. The appetite is there but eating is not possible. When you see that combination, look at the mouth before you look at the digestive tract.

The next most common sign is reduced grooming. Cats groom with the tongue, and when the mouth hurts they give it up. If the coat over the back or hindquarters has turned dull and matted within days, oral pain belongs on the list of suspects alongside joint problems. Becoming irritable or shying away from being touched can also be an expression of pain.

There is one thing you should never do: pry open a painful mouth to check. The cat may injure itself struggling, and you may get bitten. If you want a record, film the cat eating and show it at the clinic instead. Whether chewing favours one side, and at what point the cat stops, is all captured on video.

Weight loss on its own is urgent. If a cat goes several days without eating properly, fat can accumulate in the liver and cause further problems. Why it happens — there is no single cause

The cause of stomatitis is still not settled. The most widely accepted explanation is that the immune system overreacts to continuous antigenic stimulation in the mouth (bacteria and viruses), leaving the mucosa chronically ulcerated. In other words, killing bacteria alone does not solve it — which is why antibiotics alone often produce a brief improvement followed by relapse.

The factors that come up repeatedly in the research are these.

  • Feline calicivirus (FCV) — the microbe most strongly associated with the disease. In one study it was detected in 21 of 23 affected cats and in none of the controls. However, viral load did not track with lesion severity or treatment outcome.
  • FeLV and FIV — less a cause than something that worsens the outlook. FeLV-positive cats have been reported as significantly more likely to show no improvement after extraction therapy than uninfected cats.
  • Multi-cat households — risk rises markedly where several cats live together, with reports that the odds increase further with each additional cat. The usual interpretation is easier viral circulation combined with chronic stress.
  • Plaque and the oral microbiome — tartar itself is not the cause, but plaque keeps supplying the stimulus. Analyses have found the subgingival bacterial community in affected cats differs from that of healthy cats.
  • Existing periodontal disease and tooth resorption — dental radiographs of affected cats have shown widespread periodontitis in up to 93% of cases and tooth resorption in up to 66%. The mouth can look passable while the tissue below the gumline is not.

To put it plainly, stomatitis is not a disease caused by poor care. Some cats develop it despite diligent brushing, and others never do without any. That said, some of the factors above can be changed — plaque control, stress in a multi-cat home, testing and quarantine when introducing a new cat — and that is where your effort is best spent.

What happens at the clinic

When stomatitis is suspected, the workup usually runs in this order. Knowing it in advance takes some of the sting out of "why are there so many tests?"

  • 1. Conscious oral examination — pain often limits how much can be seen, so this step establishes the rough extent and the level of discomfort.
  • 2. Bloodwork and virus testing — a complete blood count and serum chemistry for overall status, plus FeLV and FIV status. As noted, that result feeds directly into the treatment plan and the prognosis discussion. Kidney values and thyroid levels are often checked alongside.
  • 3. Oral examination under anaesthesia with dental radiographs — this is the crucial step. Root disease and resorptive lesions below the gumline are invisible without imaging, and these images are what determine the extent of extraction.
  • 4. Biopsy where needed — if a lesion is unusually one-sided or proliferative in a way that raises suspicion of neoplasia or another condition, tissue is sampled.
  • 5. Scoring the severity — clinics may record disease activity with an index (such as the SDAI) so that before and after can be compared on the same scale.

The word "anaesthesia" understandably causes worry. It helps to know that in stomatitis there is very little useful information available without it — a painful mouth cannot be forced open, and radiographs require a still patient. The more productive conversation is about lowering the risk with pre-anaesthetic testing. For an older cat, working out the human-age equivalent with the cat age calculator and discussing screening for underlying disease (kidneys in particular) is worth doing.

Treatment — why extraction became the standard

This is the part owners find hardest to accept: "Why pull out healthy teeth?" The reason is straightforward. What the immune system is reacting to is the plaque on tooth surfaces and the tissue around the teeth, and extraction is currently the way to remove that stimulus at its source. Scaling only cleans the surface; plaque returns within days.

That said, the reported results differ between sources. Studies from the late 1990s and 2000s reported around 80% improvement after extraction, and some current summaries still state that partial or full-mouth extraction gives substantial improvement or complete resolution in 70-80% of cats, with 20-30% showing minimal or no response. A more recent review is more conservative: complete resolution in 28%, substantial improvement in 39%, and no response in 33%. Combined improvement lands in a similar place — about two thirds — but the share reaching an actual "cure" is lower than owners expect. The same review also reported no significant difference in overall response between full-mouth and partial extractions, which qualifies the assumption that everything must come out.

Two points from that table deserve emphasis. First, pain relief is not an afterthought. Stomatitis hurts, and controlling the pain is what allows a cat to eat, groom and recover. Second, extraction is often not the end. One source reports that even among cats that reached substantial improvement or complete resolution after extraction, about 69% required medication — antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory or analgesic — for a finite period beyond the first two postoperative weeks. It is easier to think of this as a recovery spanning months rather than a single procedure that ends it.

The drug names above are listed only so you know what options exist; they are not information an owner can use to decide on a medication or a dose. Steroids in particular can lead to other problems such as diabetes if used long-term without supervision, and must be given under veterinary prescription and monitoring. Diagnosis and treatment decisions belong in a consultation at a veterinary clinic.

What you can do at home — and what not to do

The main axis of treatment sits with the clinic, but daily care changes the outcome more than people expect. In order:

  • Make eating easy. While the mouth is painful, soak kibble thoroughly in lukewarm water or switch to wet food, and swap in a wide, shallow bowl that does not press on the whiskers. Warming food slightly releases more aroma and stimulates appetite better than serving it cold.
  • Watch water intake. A sore mouth means less drinking, which in hot weather alone can lead to dehydration and bladder problems. Check the target for your cat's weight with the pet water intake calculator, and tell your vet if actual intake is well below it.
  • Start brushing only after the pain is controlled. Putting a brush into an ulcerated mouth is not care. After recovery, begin with a finger brush or gauze, keep it brief, and do it at the same time each day to build the habit. Never use human toothpaste.
  • In a multi-cat home, reduce stress. Provide one more food bowl, water bowl and litter box than you have cats, and add hiding spots and elevated perches so the cats simply encounter each other less. When introducing a new cat, do the virus testing and keep the quarantine period.
  • Keep a record. Jotting down daily food intake, weekly weight and the degree of drooling gives your vet something far more useful than "he seems a bit better." A single line in a phone note is enough.
  • What not to do — human anti-inflammatory painkillers (ibuprofen, acetaminophen and the like) can be fatal to cats and are absolutely off-limits. Spraying oral rinses or disinfectants you read about online into the mouth is not advisable either, and re-using leftover antibiotics on your own only breeds resistance.

One last thing: stomatitis is a disease that wears owners down. Treatment is long, cats improve and relapse, and the costs are not small. But the direction is clear — lower the pain, reduce the stimulus, and re-check on schedule. When those three hold, most cats end up eating far more comfortably than before.

It is easy to lose track of what is safe to offer a cat with a sore mouth. Human foods that are dangerous to cats are collected in Foods Dogs & Cats Can't Eat. Everything here is general information — assessment and treatment decisions must be made with a veterinarian at a clinic.

Can good toothbrushing prevent stomatitis?

Brushing clearly helps, because it reduces plaque and periodontal disease, and it matters for long-term management in a cat that already has stomatitis. But stomatitis is not caused by tartar alone — it is considered multifactorial, involving an exaggerated immune response, viruses and the oral microbiome. So there is no reason to blame yourself for not brushing, and no reason to assume brushing alone makes a cat immune. Forcing a toothbrush into an already painful mouth only increases pain and resistance, so start only after the pain is under control.

Do all the teeth really have to come out? How will my cat eat?

Extraction is the treatment with the most evidence behind it, but full-mouth extraction is not automatic. A recent review reported no significant difference in overall response between cats treated with partial extractions and those treated with full-mouth extractions, so the extent is decided from dental radiographs and the spread of inflammation. As for eating, most cats do well. Cats tend to swallow food rather than chew it finely, so after recovery many manage wet food and even kibble with their gums. Appetite often improves once the pain is gone.

What if extraction does not help?

A proportion of cats do not respond. Sources put non-response at 20-30%, or about one third. In that case the vet will re-check dental radiographs for retained root fragments and discuss options such as ciclosporin and other immunomodulators, recombinant feline interferon omega, pain control, or mesenchymal stem cell therapy. FeLV-positive cats have been reported to be significantly more likely not to improve after extraction, which is why the virus test result shapes the plan from the start.

How do I tell severe bad breath from stomatitis versus ordinary tartar?

You cannot reliably tell at home, but there are clues. Tartar and periodontitis are mostly confined to the gum margin around the teeth and cause less obvious pain, while stomatitis inflames the tissue behind the molars toward the throat and hurts enough that the cat drools or cries and walks away from the food bowl. Strongly refusing to let you open the mouth is itself a pain signal. Confirmation requires an oral exam and dental radiographs, so have it checked at a veterinary clinic.
