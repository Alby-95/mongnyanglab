Can Dogs Eat Cheese?

The bottom line first: yes, but "cheese" is the wrong unit — what matters is which cheese and how much. Some types are fine in small amounts and some should never be given at all.

If milk upsets dogs, why is cheese different?

You have probably heard that milk gives dogs an upset stomach. The reason is an enzyme called lactase. Puppies produce plenty of it while nursing, but production drops sharply after weaning. So when an adult dog drinks milk, the lactose passes through the small intestine undigested and reaches the colon, where resident bacteria ferment it instead — producing gas and loose stool. That is lactose intolerance.

Cheese is easier on dogs because much of the lactose leaves during the making of it. Milk is coagulated and separated into solid curds and liquid whey, and because lactose is water-soluble, most of it drains away with the whey. Whatever remains keeps being consumed by bacteria during ageing. That means that even starting from the same milk, the longer a cheese is aged, the less lactose is left. Published composition data puts aged cheeses like cheddar and parmesan below 0.01g per 100g — essentially undetectable — while cream cheese sits around 3.6g and ricotta around 2.8g.

If that were the whole story, the answer would simply be "give aged cheese." It is not, because as ageing progresses and water is driven off, fat and sodium become more concentrated even as lactose falls. Choosing a cheese therefore means looking at lactose, fat and sodium together. A cheese low enough in lactose to avoid an upset stomach may still carry enough fat to raise the risk of pancreatitis, or enough salt to matter — while the low-fat fresh cheeses tend to be the ones with lactose still in them.

So this guide breaks "can dogs have cheese" into three narrower questions: which cheese, how much, and for which dog.

Goat cheese and cream cheese are where the sources genuinely disagree. AKC-aligned material presents soft goat cheese as one of the lower-fat, reasonable options, while other vet-reviewed guides put goat cheese, cream cheese and feta on a do-not-feed list outright. The difference usually comes down to which product each source had in mind, since fat and salt vary enormously between two things both labelled "goat cheese". Where the guidance splits, the simplest response is not to experiment — stay with the options near the top of the table.

In practice that leaves two realistic picks: low-fat cottage cheese and part-skim mozzarella. There is nothing forbidden about cheddar, but its fat and sodium mean that "one piece" has to be a much smaller piece. Three cheeses to avoid entirely

Unlike the types above, where portion size does the work, these three should be avoided regardless of amount.

  • Blue cheese, gorgonzola, roquefort and other mould-ripened cheeses
    The mould that creates the blue veining produces roquefortine C. Multiple sources classify this compound as harmful to dogs, describing vomiting, diarrhoea and restlessness at the milder end, and muscle tremors or seizures in severe cases. What they do not provide is a threshold — no source states how many grams becomes dangerous. They say only that a small amount may cause no harm, which is another way of saying you cannot draw a safe line at home. If your dog has eaten some, write down the amount and the time and call your vet.
  • Cheese containing garlic, onion, chives or herbs
    Here the problem is not the cheese but what is mixed into it. Alliums including garlic and onion can damage a dog's red blood cells and cause anaemia, and this is a risk that accumulates with repeated small exposures. Herb- and pepper-flavoured processed cheeses, garlic spreads and the cheese on a pizza all fall into this category.
  • Cheese puffs, cheese-flavoured snacks and other human packaged foods
    These carry the word cheese but are often mostly salt, oil and flavouring. Products marketed as sugar-free are a particular concern because xylitol is potentially fatal to dogs, so any human snack whose ingredient list you cannot check is best excluded entirely.

The same logic rules out cheese that arrives inside cooked dishes — pizza, lasagne, cheesy pasta. The cheese itself is not the issue; the onion, garlic, salt and oil cooked in with it are. And "I only gave them the cheese part" does not help much, because that cheese has already absorbed the seasoning around it.

How much, by body weight

The governing rule for treats is that they should stay under 10% of daily calories, and cheese runs into that ceiling faster than most things. Cheddar comes in at roughly 400kcal per 100g, so 10g is already 40kcal — and given that a 5kg dog needs somewhere in the region of 300 to 400kcal a day, 8 to 10 grams of cheese can use up nearly the whole treat budget. Halve these amounts or less for high-fat cheeses like cheddar and parmesan, and you can be slightly more relaxed with low-fat cottage cheese. What matters is not the cheese figure in isolation but the sum of everything given that day — jerky, chews, training treats and the piece a family member slipped under the table all have to fit inside the same 10%. Cheese is unusually palatable, which makes it the classic treat whose quantity creeps up during training without anyone noticing, so when using it for training it helps to portion out the day's allowance in advance and work only from that.

Your dog's daily calorie needs depend on age, neuter status and activity level as well as weight. Put the details into the dog food calculator and it will give you a daily calorie figure; 10% of that number is the whole treat budget. Decide how much of that share cheese gets, and you have a threshold far more accurate than any table.

Dogs for whom cheese is a poor fit

The same portion carries different risk depending on the dog. If any of the following applies, check with your vet before offering cheese.

  • Dogs with pancreatitis, current or past — the clearest case for avoiding it. Pancreatitis is a condition classically triggered by fatty food, and sources state plainly that dogs with a history of it should not be given cheese. Once a dog has had an episode, the risk of another never fully goes away.
  • Overweight dogs or dogs on a weight plan — cheese is extremely calorie-dense for its volume. A fingernail-sized piece can match a spoonful of kibble in calories, so it eats the budget much faster than it feels like it should.
  • Dogs with kidney or heart disease — sodium often has to be managed, so it is better not to add to it casually. Salty cheeses like parmesan and processed slices are out.
  • Dogs with habitually loose stool or sensitive stomachs — even a little lactose can provoke a reaction. Start with a very small amount even of the gentler types like cottage cheese.
  • Puppies — their digestive systems are still settling, so they react more readily to new foods. Cheese is often used as a training reward at exactly this age; start with a grain-of-rice-sized piece and watch for a day.
  • Dogs with a dairy allergy — separate from lactose intolerance, some dogs react to milk protein. It tends to show up as itching, ear inflammation or paw-licking rather than diarrhoea, which makes it easy to miss. If those signs keep recurring, it is worth reading up on itchy skin in dogs as well.

Whatever the case, the rule is to introduce new foods one at a time. Give cheese alongside another new treat and you lose the ability to identify the culprit if something goes wrong.

Giving it safely — and using it for medication

In practice, this is the order to follow.

  • 1. Choose the type first. Default to low-fat cottage cheese or part-skim mozzarella, and check the label for garlic, onion, herbs or flavourings.
  • 2. Start with a single piece the size of a grain of rice or a pea. Then watch stool, flatulence and general condition for about 24 hours. If nothing changes, you can work up to the amounts in the table.
  • 3. Cut it small. A folded slice handed over whole can be swallowed without chewing and become a choking hazard, particularly in small dogs.
  • 4. Subtract from that day's other treats. On a cheese day, cut back on training treats or chews to keep the total in range.
  • 5. Skip cheese that has been sitting out. It spoils easily in summer, and mouldy cheese brings you back to the mould-toxin problem above. If it looks questionable to you, do not give it to your dog.

Where cheese genuinely earns its place is at medication time. A dog who spits out a tablet offered plainly will often swallow it wrapped in cheese, and several sources mention cheese as a pill-hiding aid for exactly this reason. Two caveats. First, the calcium in dairy can interfere with the absorption of some medications, so it is worth asking when the prescription is written whether wrapping it in cheese is fine. Second, a daily medication means daily cheese — and at that frequency, purpose-made pill-hiding treats are the better answer on calories and sodium.

Can I give my dog a whole slice of processed cheese?

A whole slice is not a good idea, and the reason is sodium rather than lactose. Processed slices are made by adding salt and emulsifiers to natural cheese, so they run high in sodium for their weight, and depending on the brand a single slice of about 18 to 20 grams can contain more than 200mg. Set against how little sodium a small dog actually needs in a day, one slice can approach a whole day's worth on its own. If you are going to give any, tear off a piece the size of a fingernail rather than handing over the slice, and cut back on other treats that day. Folding a slice in half and letting your dog swallow it whole is also a choking risk, so it is best avoided.

My dog just ate some blue cheese. What should I do?

Note how much was eaten and when, and call your vet right away. The mould that gives blue cheese its veining produces a compound called roquefortine C, which is described as harmful to dogs and can cause vomiting, diarrhoea and restlessness, and in more serious cases muscle tremors or seizures. What the sources do not give is a threshold — they say only that a small amount may cause no harm, without stating how much is too much. An unclear threshold means this is not something you can judge at home, so if your dog is trembling or unsteady on their feet, seek treatment immediately rather than waiting. Do not try to make your dog vomit at home.

My dog has diarrhoea after eating cheese. Is this lactose intolerance?

Very likely. Dogs produce much less lactase once they are weaned, so undigested lactose travels down to the colon where bacteria ferment it, producing gas and loose stool. It usually shows up within a few hours to a day as diarrhoea, flatulence or a bloated belly. In most cases it settles within a day once the dairy stops, so cut out cheese and make sure your dog can drink freely. But if the diarrhoea lasts more than 24 hours, or comes with blood, repeated vomiting or lethargy, something else may be going on — and if a large amount of high-fat cheese was eaten at once, pancreatitis is also possible, so have your dog checked.

Are cheese treats made for dogs safe to give freely?

No. Products made for pets are often lower in sodium or lactose than human cheese, which makes them the safer choice, but they are still treats with fat and calories. The rule that treats should stay under 10% of daily calories applies to them exactly as it does to anything else. Cheese sticks in particular can be higher in calories than they look, so check the label and break one into several servings rather than giving the whole stick at once.
