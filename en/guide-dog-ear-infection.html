Ear Infections in Dogs: Symptoms, Causes and Summer Care

The bottom line first: sour-smelling ears, more black or yellow discharge, and repeated head shaking usually point to an ear infection. Because the right medication depends on whether bacteria, yeast or mites are behind it, don't guess at home — have a vet check the discharge.

Signs that suggest an ear infection

Otitis externa is inflammation of the ear canal lining — the passage running from the ear opening down to the eardrum. Your dog can't tell you it hurts, so it comes out as behavior instead.

  • Frequent, forceful head shaking — an attempt to clear fluid and debris out of the canal. This is the most common early sign.
  • Scratching at the ear with a hind paw, or rubbing that side of the face along the floor or sofa.
  • Odor — a distinctly sour or musty smell. Simply lifting the ear flap and smelling tells you a lot.
  • A change in discharge — thick black or yellow wax, or brown coffee-ground looking material. Dark discharge often points toward yeast or ear mites.
  • Redness and swelling inside the ear, flinching or pulling away when you touch it, unusual sensitivity around the ear.
  • One ear far worse than the other is common, so always compare both sides.

Left untreated for a long time, the skin lining the canal thickens and the canal itself narrows, which makes medication harder to get where it needs to go. Severe chronic cases can end up needing surgery, so a little scratching is not something to keep waiting out.

Why humid summer weather makes it worse

A dog's ear canal isn't straight like ours — it runs down vertically and then turns horizontally, in an "L" shape. That shape protects the deeper ear, but it also means water and wax that get in don't drain out easily. Add a humid rainy season and the canal stays warm and damp, which is exactly the environment bacteria and yeast multiply in. That's why infections so often show up after a bath, a swim, or a walk in the rain.

Some dogs are working against their own anatomy. Breeds with large, floppy or hairy ears — cocker spaniels, poodles, Old English sheepdogs and similar — get less airflow through the canal and are more prone to infection. For these dogs it's worth lifting the ear and checking often through the summer.

The real cause may not be the ear at all

The most frustrating thing about ear infections is that they improve on medication and then return once it stops. That usually isn't a care failure — it's because the ear infection is the result of another problem. Allergies are the most common driver: veterinary sources report allergies are involved in as many as 43% of ear infection cases, and that 65% to 80% of dogs with food allergies develop ear infections. Hormonal disease such as hypothyroidism, foreign material like grass seeds, ear mites (especially in puppies), and stopping medication early because things look better are other frequent reasons.

So for a dog with recurring infections, treating only the ear isn't enough. Noting the skin-related signs alongside it — paw licking, redness on the belly or armpits, more eye discharge — and bringing that to your vet makes the underlying cause much easier to find. If you're reviewing diet, start by tightening up treats and table food, and check Foods Dogs & Cats Can't Eat for what shouldn't be on the menu in the first place.

What to do at home — and what never to do

At the clinic, the ear is examined with a magnifying otoscope and the discharge is looked at under a microscope. That's because bacteria, yeast and mites need completely different medications, and stubborn chronic cases may even need culture and sensitivity testing. Picking a medication yourself tends to muddy the diagnosis more than it helps.

  • Don't — push cotton swabs deep into the canal (this packs wax further in and risks the eardrum). Don't use irritating home remedies like vinegar, alcohol or hydrogen peroxide. Don't reuse leftover drops or ointments. Don't stop the medication early because symptoms eased.
  • Do — use the cleaner your vet prescribed exactly as directed, wiping only the visible outer part with a cotton pad. Dry around the ears thoroughly after baths and swimming. Let floppy ears air out by lifting them for a moment during rest. Finish the full course and keep the recheck appointment.

Senior dogs recover more slowly from the same problem and are more likely to have other conditions layered on top, so having them seen early is safer. Can I treat my dog's ear infection at home?

The medication that works depends entirely on what is causing the infection — bacteria, yeast, or ear mites all need different treatment, so guessing without a microscopic look at the discharge can make things worse. Some cleaners are also unsafe if the eardrum is damaged, which is another reason a veterinarian needs to look inside the canal first. Avoid irritating home remedies such as vinegar or alcohol, and never push a cotton swab deep into the ear. What you can do at home is use the cleaner and medication your vet prescribed, exactly as directed and for the full course, and keep the ears dry after baths and swimming.

Why does my dog's ear infection keep coming back?

Ear infections are often a symptom of something else, so if the underlying cause is not addressed the infection returns as soon as the medication stops. Allergies are the most common driver: veterinary sources report that allergies are involved in as many as 43% of ear infection cases, and that 65% to 80% of dogs with food allergies develop ear infections. Hormonal conditions such as hypothyroidism, the shape and hairiness of the ear canal, foreign material like grass seeds, and stopping medication early once symptoms improve are other common reasons. If it keeps recurring, ask your vet to look beyond the ear at skin, allergy and endocrine causes.
