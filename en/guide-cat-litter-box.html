How to Choose a Cat Litter Box

The bottom line first: a litter box succeeds or fails on size, number and how often it is scooped — not on design or brand. Get those three right and most cats will use it whether it has a lid or not.

Why people start shopping for a new box

There are usually three reasons. Either a cat has just arrived and there is nothing in the house yet, or the current box looks too small, or — most urgently — the cat has started going outside the box. That last one is the most common reason and the one handled wrong most often.

So let us be clear about one thing up front. Urinating outside the box is not revenge. Cats do not urinate to get back at people. What they do is look for another place when they are in pain, when the box is uncomfortable, or when something frightening happened there. That is why the order matters: sometimes the right move before buying a box is a trip to the clinic. Cystitis, urinary stones, kidney disease, arthritis and constipation all present as "won't use the box". In particular, if a male cat gets into position and strains with nothing coming out, passes tiny amounts over and over, or cries at the box, that can be a urethral obstruction, and that is an emergency you cannot leave until tomorrow.

Once a clinic has ruled out a medical cause, it becomes an environment problem — and most environment problems are solved not by an expensive product but by a big enough box, enough boxes, and daily scooping. The rest of this article follows that order. One related thing worth checking alongside the box: how much your cat drinks. Low water intake concentrates the urine and raises the risk of bladder disease. You can work out a target for your cat's weight with the pet water intake calculator.

Three things decided before you buy anything — number, size, location

Half the outcome is settled before a product is chosen. How many boxes, where, and how big matters far more than which model. These are also the three points veterinary guidelines return to again and again.

The point missed most often is size. Ready-made boxes, especially covered ones, frequently fall short of the 1.5× standard. A typical 5kg domestic shorthair is roughly 45–50cm long, which means the inside of the box needs to be close to 70cm — and products that big are surprisingly rare. That is why a large plastic storage tub, sometimes with one side cut down, is such a common recommendation. It is cheap and it is genuinely big enough. If you can live with how it looks, it is the most reliable option available.

The "n+1" rule is a convention built from clinical experience rather than a figure proven in a large trial, and some people point out that the evidence behind it is thin. Still, nothing gets worse by adding a box, and adding one often makes an elimination problem disappear. For the cost, it is worth following.

Covered, open or top-entry — what the research actually found

The lid is the most-asked question, and there is a study worth knowing about. Twenty-seven healthy cats (aged three months to fifteen years) were given a covered and an uncovered box at the same time for two weeks. The result: about 70% showed no preference, 15% preferred covered and 15% preferred uncovered. No statistically meaningful group preference emerged.

But two conditions in that study matter. First, the boxes used were very large — about 1.5 times the length of the cat. Second, they were cleaned daily. Commercial covered boxes are typically tighter than that, and because a lid keeps the smell down, people put off scooping. In other words, the problem is less the hood itself than the cramped space and the false reassurance it creates. Consistent with that, larger cats in the same study tended toward the uncovered box.

The summary is this: if your cat is using the box happily right now, the shape is a matter of taste. If elimination outside the box has already started, opening the lid comes before agonising over models — because the fix is to widen the options, not narrow them.

Automatic boxes — what to know before buying one

This is the fastest-growing category and the one that calls for the most care. The upside is real: waste is cleared right after use, and models that log visit counts and weight through an app genuinely help catch early signals of kidney disease or bladder trouble. A sudden change in how often a cat urinates is useful information at the clinic.

There have also been safety reports. On some rotating self-cleaning models, owners have reported the sensor failing to detect a cat inside, with incidents covered in the media, and several brands were pulled from online sale. These reports centre on lower-cost models and have not produced an industry-wide official recall. Because sources disagree sharply on how serious the risk is, this article will not declare them safe or unsafe — it just lists the conditions to avoid.

  • Not for kittens. Most models are built to detect a certain minimum weight, and a light kitten may not register. Check the manufacturer's stated minimum weight and age.
  • Careful with senior and arthritic cats. A stiff cat may not get out before the cycle begins. Check whether the delay before cleaning can be lengthened.
  • Always keep a normal box too. Some cats simply will not enter because of the noise or movement. An automatic box as the only box leaves no fallback.
  • Litter choice narrows. Most require a strongly clumping bentonite litter. If you have been using tofu or pellet litter you will have to change that too — which means the box and the litter change at once, and that raises the chance of refusal. Our guide to choosing cat litter covers the trade-offs.
  • Check for a manual lock. There will be times you need to pause it — illness, visitors, a cat in recovery.

If you are unsure whether your cat counts as senior, converting the age helps. Cats are generally treated as middle-aged from about seven and senior from around eleven; the cat age calculator gives a rough human-year equivalent. From that stage onward, entry height and accessibility matter far more than shape or features.

An eight-point checklist

Working through these beats sorting by brand ranking. Points 1 and 2 in particular cannot be fixed after purchase.

  • 1. Is the interior length at least 1.5× your cat? Read the internal floor dimensions, not the external ones on the listing. Covered models lose noticeable space to wall thickness. If your cat hunches, keeps their front paws on the rim, or eliminates against the wall, the box is too small.
  • 2. Does the entry height suit your cat's body? A young cat handles 15–20cm easily, but an arthritic cat or one over eleven does better with 10cm or a lowered side. Feline arthritis hides well — often the only clue is that they have stopped jumping as much.
  • 3. Are the walls tall enough? Low entry with high remaining walls is the ideal combination. For cats who stand to urinate or dig energetically, walls of 20cm or more cut down scatter and leaks.
  • 4. Is the material smooth and durable? Rough or easily scratched plastic holds urine in the grooves, and a box that smells will be avoided even after washing. Plan on replacing the box itself every one to two years.
  • 5. Does the scoop match the litter? Tofu and pellet litters need a wide-slotted scoop, bentonite a fine one. If the included scoop is wrong, daily cleaning becomes a chore — and chores get postponed.
  • 6. Have you thought about a mat? Most of the litter that ends up on the floor rode out on paws. A mat in front of the box often outperforms switching to a covered model.
  • 7. In a multi-cat home, plan the routes too. Avoid spots that can only be reached past another box, and dead-end corners that are easy to guard. A lower-ranking cat who cannot reach a box will hold it, and holding it leads to cystitis and constipation. Warning signs are covered in cat cystitis — symptoms and prevention.
  • 8. Check the real internal size of furniture-style boxes. Cabinet-style units look large from outside while the tray inside is small. Look up the dimensions of the tray, not the cabinet.

Some features add cost without adding much. Deodorising functions and antibacterial coatings do not replace daily scooping, and strongly scented deodorisers are a common reason cats start avoiding a box. What smells clean to a person does not necessarily smell that way to a cat.

How many litter boxes should I have?

The standard used in veterinary guidelines is one per cat plus one: two boxes for one cat, three for two cats. That number comes from long clinical experience rather than a controlled trial, but an extra box has no downside, so it is worth following. What matters more than the count is the placement. Three boxes lined up in one corner function as a single box from the cat's point of view. Spread them across different rooms and different floors so there is somewhere else to go when one is dirty or another cat is guarding it. In a multi-storey home, at least one box per floor is the rule.

Is a covered litter box better than an open one?

In a study that gave 27 healthy cats both a covered and an uncovered box for two weeks, about 70% showed no preference either way, while 15% favoured the covered box and 15% the uncovered one. The lid itself was not a decisive factor for most cats. But note the conditions: the boxes used were large, about 1.5 times the length of the cat, and they were cleaned daily. Commercial covered boxes are usually smaller than that, and because a lid holds the smell in, people tend to put off scooping. So the honest summary is not that hoods are bad, but that a hood is a matter of taste as long as the box is big enough and cleaned every day. If a cat has already started going outside the box, opening it up is the safer first move.

Are automatic self-cleaning litter boxes safe?

For an adult cat that has already adjusted to one, they are a convenient tool. There is a caveat, though. Owners have reported incidents in which the sensor on a rotating self-cleaning box failed to detect a cat inside, and some products were pulled from online sale. Those reports centre on particular low-cost models and have not led to an industry-wide official recall, so risk assessments differ from source to source. Erring toward safety, they are hard to recommend for kittens light enough that the sensor may not register them, for senior or arthritic cats who cannot get out quickly, or for cats frightened by enclosed spaces. If you do use one, check the manual lock and the weight-detection method, and never make an automatic box the only box in the house.

My cat has started going outside the box. Will a new box fix it?

See a veterinarian first. Urinating outside the box is not laziness or spite; very often it is a medical problem. Cystitis, urinary stones, kidney disease, arthritis and constipation all show up as the same behaviour. In particular, a male cat straining in position with no urine coming out, passing small amounts repeatedly, or crying at the box may have a urethral obstruction, which is an emergency measured in hours. Only after a clinic has ruled out a medical cause does changing the setup make sense. After that the order is: scoop more often, go bigger, take the lid off, move the location, add another box. Change one thing at a time, roughly two weeks apart, or you will never know which change worked.

After it arrives — litter depth, scooping, and switching over

A good box run badly is no better than a cheap one. Conversely, an ordinary tub run well works for most cats. Three things carry the load.

  • Litter depth around 5cm. The usual advice is 3–5cm, a little deeper for enthusiastic diggers. Sources vary from about 3cm all the way to 7cm, so there is no single right number. Judge by behaviour rather than the figure: if digging and covering happen naturally, it is deep enough. If paws scrape the bottom, add more. Too deep is also a thing — some cats dislike sinking in.
  • Scoop clumps once or twice a day. This is very nearly the whole job. In the study above, daily cleaning mattered more than whether the box had a lid. Scooping daily also means you see the size and number of urine clumps and the state of the stool, which is how kidney disease, cystitis and constipation get caught early. That is one advantage an automatic box takes away.
  • Full changes depend on the litter. With clumping bentonite, most people scoop daily, top up what is lost, and empty and wash the box every two to four weeks. Tofu, pellet and crystal litters follow their own instructions. When washing, skip strong detergents and bleach — mild dish soap and water, rinsed well and dried completely, is enough. Ammonia-based cleaners smell like urine and can actually invite a cat to eliminate in that spot.

When switching to a new box, do not remove the old one first. That is the single most common way the switch fails. Put the new one beside the old one and give it a few days; the cat will choose. Once they are clearly using the new one, retire the old. If the new box seems strange to them, moving a scoopful of used litter across leaves a familiar scent. And avoid changing the box and the litter at the same time — two changes at once raises the chance of refusal and leaves you unable to tell which one caused a problem.

Finally, keep notes. Writing down the date you changed the box, the date you changed the litter, and the date urination frequency shifted turns into real help at the clinic later. "She's only gone twice a day since Tuesday" is a surprisingly specific clue during an examination. Everything here is general guidance — if the posture looks wrong or the volume of urine changes suddenly, do not put it down to the box. A proper diagnosis comes from your veterinarian.
