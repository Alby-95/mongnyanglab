Dog Swimming Safety: How to Do Water Play Right

The bottom line first: not every dog can swim, and trouble shows up more often after the swim than during it.

Not every dog can actually swim

Because of the phrase "doggy paddle," many owners assume a dog placed in water will instinctively swim. That is only half true. Nearly every dog will paddle with its front legs when it hits the water, but whether that paddling keeps the body afloat depends entirely on build. If the front legs churn while the hindquarters sink, the body tips to a 45-degree angle and the nose and mouth end up under the surface with the front paws still going. To an owner watching from the bank it looks like swimming. It can be the start of drowning.

Some body types are clearly disadvantaged in water. Brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds such as French Bulldogs, Pugs and Shih Tzus struggle to hold their heads high enough to breathe for long, and their heavy chest muscle pulls the front end down. Breeds with short legs and long backs, like Dachshunds and Corgis, generate little propulsion and load the spine while trying. At the other end, dogs bred for water work — Retrievers, Poodles, Portuguese Water Dogs — have webbed feet and coats that shed water, and find it far easier.

A "disadvantaged" build in that table does not mean staying away from water altogether. Splashing in water they can stand up in and swimming where their feet don't touch are completely different activities, and if the goal is cooling off in summer, the first one is plenty. Accidents usually happen at the moment someone thinks "mine will be fine" and wades one step deeper.

Water intoxication: the risk most owners never hear about

Ask about swimming accidents and most people picture drowning. The one that slips past owners is water intoxication, or hyponatremia. Swallowing too much water in a short window dilutes the sodium in the blood, and the body responds by pulling fluid into the cells. Most organs can tolerate some swelling, but the brain is enclosed in bone and has nowhere to expand, so pressure builds and neurological signs follow.

The catch is that dogs rarely get there by drinking. Almost every real case involves water swallowed without the dog noticing during play: repeatedly swimming out for a ball or disc with the mouth open, swimming with a low head that pushes water past the muzzle, or biting at the stream from a hose or sprinkler. Published veterinary sources consistently name the same high-risk group — small dogs, lean dogs with little body fat, and high-drive dogs who won't stop playing even when tired. One UK emergency practice describes a healthy spaniel who collapsed after playing in a lake and died within seven hours despite treatment. Rare, but fast.

Prevention is simpler than it sounds. Call your dog out of the water every 10 to 15 minutes. Use the break to offer a toilet opportunity, which gives the body time to shed the extra fluid. Choose flat, disc-shaped toys over balls that force the mouth wide open, and skip the hose-biting game entirely. If you're curious what a normal daily water intake looks like in the first place, our pet water intake calculator gives you a figure by body weight. Water intoxication is what happens when that figure is blown past in a matter of minutes.

The 24 hours after the swim matter more

This is where the biggest misunderstanding lives. A dog takes in some water, coughs for a moment, seems fine, and everyone goes home relaxed. But water that reached the lungs causes problems over hours. It interferes with oxygen exchange and washes away the surfactant lining the air sacs, and if the water carried debris and bacteria — creek water, sea water — it can progress to aspiration pneumonia. The signs typically begin hours later, not immediately.

Sources disagree on the vocabulary here. Some pet articles borrow the term "dry drowning" from human media, while many clinicians argue the term is medically imprecise and should be dropped. The practical conclusion is the same either way: a dog who swallowed a lot of water or went under, even briefly, needs to be watched through the evening and the following day.

The risk changes with where you swim

The same swim carries different hazards depending on the water. What worries you at a creek is not what worries you at the beach.

The most frightening item on that list is blue-green algae, or cyanobacteria. Cornell's veterinary college states plainly that there is no antidote for blue-green algae poisoning and that it can rapidly become fatal. Signs can appear within minutes to several hours and include vomiting, diarrhoea, weakness, muscle tremors, breathing difficulty, seizures and sudden death. Blooms favour stagnant water in warm weather after a dry spell, and the tell-tale signs are blue-green or red-brown mats, foam or scum on the surface, and a rotting-vegetation smell. Don't even let your dog wade in such water — anything that coats the fur gets swallowed later during grooming. If your dog has already been in, rinse the whole coat with clean water immediately and call your vet.

Standing water carries one more concern: leptospirosis. It spreads through water contaminated with the urine of rodents and other wildlife, and it can be passed to humans. In many vaccination programmes the leptospirosis vaccine is recommended selectively based on a dog's lifestyle, so if your summers involve regular trips to creeks and reservoirs, it's worth raising with your vet. A before, during and after checklist

Run through this before you head out and you'll avoid most of what goes wrong.

  • A life jacket isn't optional for a disadvantaged build. Look for flotation that supports the neck under the chin, at least two chest and belly straps, and a back handle strong enough to lift your dog one-handed. Size by chest girth, not weight, or it will slip off in the water.
  • Don't swim right after a meal. Vigorous activity on a full stomach risks vomiting, and in large breeds you also have bloat to think about. Leave at least one to two hours.
  • Test water temperature with your forearm, not your hand. Creek water is colder than it looks, and small dogs, puppies and seniors lose heat quickly. The moment your dog starts shivering, call them out.
  • Bring separate drinking water. Letting your dog drink the water they're playing in is the starting point for both infection and water intoxication. Offer clean water in small amounts during breaks.
  • Keep to a 10–15 minute play-and-rest rhythm. Dogs don't stop themselves. That judgement is yours.
  • Rinse the whole body afterwards. Salt, chlorine, sand and algae all irritate skin, and whatever stays in the coat is eventually swallowed during grooming.
  • Dry the ears and between the toes. A wet, folded ear is an ideal environment for bacteria and yeast, and ear infections spike in swimming season for drop-eared breeds. See our guide to canine ear infections for the routine.
  • Check temperature and general condition. Being in water does not rule out heatstroke. In midsummer, watch for excessive panting and unusually red gums as well.

One more thing worth remembering: the car on the way home is hot. A dog already worn out from swimming can overheat quickly sitting in a warm vehicle. The early signs of heatstroke and what to do first are laid out step by step in our dog heatstroke guide.

Signs that mean go to the vet now

If any of the following applies, don't watch and wait at home.

  • A cough after swimming that won't settle or is newly appeared, or foam from the nose or mouth
  • Resting respiratory rate above 35 per minute, breathing with abdominal effort, or holding the neck stretched forward
  • Gums that look pale or bluish
  • A staggering gait, glazed eyes, or a visibly distended belly
  • Repeated vomiting, or being limp and unresponsive after the swim
  • Any contact with water you suspect had an algal bloom — call even with no symptoms
  • Seizures or reduced consciousness — emergency care immediately

When you go, bring the details: what kind of water it was (creek, sea, pool), how long your dog was in it, whether you saw them swallowing water, and when the signs began. A photo of the water itself helps too, if you have one.

Finally, everything here is general guidance. Diagnosis and treatment must come from a veterinarian. The same sign can mean different things depending on your dog's age, build and existing conditions. When you're unsure, a phone call to the clinic beats reading one more article.

Can a dog who is afraid of water be taught to swim?

If your dog's build allows it, yes — slowly. What you must never do is throw a dog into the water. It cements the fear, and a panicking dog swallows a great deal of water, which can lead to aspiration. Start in water that only covers the ankles, use treats, and build up to belly depth over several days. Then, with a life jacket on, support your dog's belly with your hands and let them float briefly. If your dog still hates it, there is no reason to force the issue — splashing around in shallow water cools them down just as well.

Water got into my dog's ears after swimming. Can I clean them with a cotton swab?

Pushing a cotton swab into the ear canal is not recommended. A dog's ear canal bends in an L shape, so a swab tends to push debris and moisture further in and can injure the eardrum. Wipe only the visible outer ear flap with a dry towel or cotton pad to absorb moisture. For the canal itself, use an ear cleaner recommended by your vet, massage the base of the ear gently, and let your dog shake the rest out. If your dog starts scratching the ears, shaking the head, or the ears begin to smell, have them checked for an ear infection.

My dog swallowed sea water. Is that dangerous?

A few mouthfuls usually pass as nothing worse than loose stool. The problem is repeated swallowing — fetching a ball through breaking waves, for example. A large salt load raises blood sodium sharply and can cause vomiting, diarrhoea, intense thirst, wobbling and seizures. This is an emergency in the opposite direction from water intoxication. Always bring fresh drinking water to the beach and offer it often, so your dog never tries to quench thirst with sea water. If you see repeated vomiting, lethargy or an unsteady gait after a swim, go to the vet.

Is pool chlorine harmful to dogs?

Most sources agree that the chlorine level in a properly maintained pool is not high enough to poison a dog during a short swim. The realistic problems are letting your dog drink the water continuously, and irritation of the skin, eyes and ears. Conversely, an unmaintained private pool or a body of standing water can be more dangerous than a chlorinated pool because of bacteria and algae. Either way, rinse your dog off with clean water afterwards and dry the ears and between the toes. If your dog has a history of skin disease, discuss swimming frequency with your vet.
