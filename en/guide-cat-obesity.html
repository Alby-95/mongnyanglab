Cat Weight Loss — Why Crash Dieting Is the One Thing You Must Not Do

The bottom line first: a cat should lose no more than 0.5 to 2% of its body weight per week. Cut the food too hard and what you damage is not the fat but the liver.

Is your cat actually overweight?

Feline obesity is one of the problems owners notice last. You see the same face every day so the change never stands out, the extra weight often looks endearing, and "he's just big-boned" is a fairly convincing explanation. Yet Cornell's Feline Health Center reports that roughly half of the cats seen at veterinary clinics are overweight or obese. In other words, the body that looks perfectly ordinary is frequently already above average.

The definition itself is simple. Cornell treats a body weight 20% or more above normal as obesity. The difficulty is that skeletal frames differ, so "normal weight" is not a fixed number of kilograms. Two domestic shorthairs in the same household can have ideal weights of 3.2 kg and 5 kg. That is why practices use the body condition score (BCS), a nine-point scale based on what you feel and see rather than what the scale says — which means you can apply it at home.

Check from three directions. First, sweep a flat palm along both sides of the ribcage. The ribs should feel about like the bones on the back of your hand; if you have to press in to find them, there is fat over them. Second, look down from above: there should be a slight narrowing behind the ribs. Third, look from the side: the underline of the belly should rise gently from chest to hind legs. If it runs parallel to the floor or sags, the cat is carrying too much.

One point causes confusion regularly. The loose, swinging flap of skin low on a cat's belly — the primordial pouch — is normal anatomy, not evidence of obesity. Lean cats have it too. Judge by the ribs and the waist, not by the pouch.

If you have no idea what the ideal weight would be, current weight plus BCS gives a usable starting point. A widely used conversion treats BCS 6 as about 10% above ideal, 7 as about 20% above, and 8 as about 30% above. A 5 kg cat at BCS 7 therefore has an ideal weight somewhere near 4.2 kg. This is only a starting estimate — the actual target is worth confirming at the clinic.

What extra weight actually causes

"A bit chubby never hurt anyone" is a reasonable instinct, so it is worth spelling out what obesity brings with it. The conditions Cornell lists as potentially aggravated by excess weight are osteoarthritis, hip dysplasia, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular strain. The joint problem is the hardest for an owner to notice, because a cat in pain does not limp — it simply moves less. It stops going up the cat tree, walks around obstacles instead of jumping them, grooms less so the fur along the back mats. Read as "she's calmed down with age", that pain goes untreated.

Diabetes is more direct. More fat tissue means insulin resistance, and sustained insulin resistance breaks glucose control. The encouraging part is that feline diabetes can go into remission — controlled without insulin — when weight comes off. That differs from the canine disease, and it is one reason weight management is not a cosmetic matter.

There are quieter consequences too. A heavy cat cannot fold itself up to groom properly. The back, rump and tail base get greasy and scurfy, and skin inflammation can follow. Crouching in the litter tray becomes awkward as well; if your cat is prone to constipation, weight may be one strand of the cause.

Here it is worth being honest about where the evidence disagrees. An analysis of 2,609 cats (2,281 with survival data) found that while obesity was associated with certain chronic illnesses, it did not shorten average lifespan. Cats scoring BCS 6 to 8 actually lived longer, and lean cats at BCS 3 to 4 had markedly shorter survival. That result should not be taken at face value, though. Lean cats are frequently lean because they are ill — kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, cancer — and the study measured survival not from birth but from the point of the highest recorded BCS, while excluding BCS 1–2 for small sample size. It is not a reason to let a cat gain weight. The fair summary is that there is little disagreement that obesity increases joint, metabolic and skin problems, and genuine disagreement about its effect on lifespan.

Why cats gain — usually one of three routes

The paths to a heavy cat are more predictable than you would expect. Most cases are one of the following, or several at once.

  • Portions left unchanged after neutering — resting energy needs fall while appetite rises. The Purina Institute recommends cutting intake by around 30% after the surgery. As neutering is typically done around six months, failing to adjust at that point commonly produces a BCS 6–7 cat by one or two years old.
  • Free feeding — keeping the bowl topped up means nobody knows how much was eaten in a day, which makes management impossible from the start. Cornell likewise advises scheduled, measured meals instead of free feeding.
  • Treats, and the accumulation of "just a little" — a cat's daily calorie requirement is very small by human standards. A few flakes of tuna and two lickable treats can account for a substantial share of a 4 kg cat's day. In a household of several people, small separate contributions add up to double the intended amount.

Indoor living, middle age, and competition at the bowl in multi-cat homes all add to this. Conversely, if intake has not increased but weight is climbing, hypothyroidism or medication such as steroids may be involved, so it is worth a check before starting any diet. And eating more while losing weight points the other way entirely — toward diabetes, hyperthyroidism or chronic kidney disease — and calls for investigation, not a diet.

How many calories a day — the maths and a table

Weight-loss portions are calculated from the target (ideal) weight, not the current weight. Miss this and the diet stalls no matter how much you cut. The American Animal Hospital Association sets it out in two steps.

  • Step 1 — resting energy requirement (RER): RER (kcal/day) = 70 × (ideal body weight in kg)0.75
    A simpler formula, 30 × weight (kg) + 70, exists for animals between 2 and 25 kg, but it tends to underestimate small animals, so the first equation suits cats better.
  • Step 2 — the weight-loss target: AAHA notes that feeding 80% of the ideal-weight RER is effective and well tolerated for weight management.

If the arithmetic is a nuisance, use the table below directly. Note only that the left column is the target weight, not today's weight.

Next, convert calories into grams of food. Find the metabolisable energy (ME) on the packaging — kcal per kg or per 100 g — and divide. For a target weight of 4 kg at 158 kcal a day, with a dry food at 380 kcal per 100 g, that is about 41 g daily. To see how today's portion compares with a maintenance amount, run the numbers through the cat food calculator first.

One practical point matters more than it sounds. VCA advises giving the amount in grams, weighed on a kitchen scale, rather than in cups or cans. A measuring cup can vary by more than 20% depending on how it is filled, and during a diet that error alone can decide the outcome. Keep treats within 10% of the daily calorie total and subtract that amount from the food. Treats added on top break the whole calculation.

How slowly — and why starving a cat is dangerous

This is the most important section of the article. Cats tolerate rapid weight loss and fasting far worse than dogs or people. When body fat is mobilised all at once it overwhelms the liver, fat accumulates inside the liver cells and they stop functioning: hepatic lipidosis, or fatty liver. VCA states that it can begin after three to four consecutive days of eating little or nothing, and that the risk rises sharply in a cat that was already overweight. Treatment generally means a feeding tube into the oesophagus or stomach with feeds three to five times a day, and an average of six to seven weeks of tube feeding before normal eating resumes. Left untreated rapidly and aggressively, it can be fatal.

Hence the ceiling on the rate. Cornell recommends 1 to 2% of body weight per week; VCA gives the same 1 to 2% but adds that closer to 0.5% may be more appropriate for a morbidly obese cat; the Association for Pet Obesity Prevention widens the range to 0.5 to 2% per week. The lower bound differs between sources, but they agree on the 2% ceiling. For a 6 kg cat that is 30 to 120 g a week — a change you will not see by looking.

At that rate it takes a long time. Taking a 6 kg cat down to 4.8 kg at 1% a week runs to twenty weeks or more. Close to half a year, and that is the normal speed. If the weight has visibly dropped in three weeks, that is not the diet going well — it is going too fast.

APOP puts it plainly: if the cat stops eating or eats much less than usual, pause the plan. A falling appetite during a diet is not willpower arriving; it is a warning. This most often happens when a new food does not suit the cat, and waiting it out on the theory that hunger will win is the most dangerous choice available.

The figures here assume a healthy adult cat. Kittens, pregnant or nursing cats, senior cats and cats with kidney, heart or liver disease or diabetes need entirely different calculations and targets. Any real weight-loss plan should be set with a veterinarian.

Doing it at home

With the numbers settled, here is the order of operations.

  • 1. Record the starting point — current weight, BCS, the food and daily amount, and every treat. Working from memory means never knowing afterwards what worked.
  • 2. End free feeding and move to meals — set a daily total and split it into two to four servings. More frequent meals reduce the sense of hunger without changing the total. For a cat that wakes you at night, placing the last meal late helps.
  • 3. Do not cut calories in one step — move from the current intake to the target over one to two weeks. If you are changing foods, transition gradually over seven to ten days.
  • 4. Use wet food — Cornell recommends canned food for weight loss: higher in protein, lower in carbohydrate, and about 90% water, so the same calories occupy more volume. Water intake improves as a side effect (the water intake calculator gives a daily target).
  • 5. Make eating harder work — a puzzle feeder or foraging mat slows eating and adds activity. Splitting the ration between several bowls around the house achieves something similar.
  • 6. Short play, often — APOP advises brief play sessions the cat enjoys rather than hitting a set duration, adjusted for age, fitness, pain and mobility. Asking a heavy cat to make big jumps is hard on the joints.
  • 7. Weigh every two weeks under the same conditions — VCA advises weighing at least every other week until the goal is reached. A baby scale works, or weigh yourself holding the cat and subtract. Keep the conditions constant, such as before the morning meal.
  • 8. Reaching the target is where it starts — keep feeding the weight-loss amount and the cat keeps losing; go back to the old amount and the weight returns. At goal weight, recalculate for maintenance and keep checking monthly.

One last thing, about the human side. Cats on a diet usually ask more insistently: crying at dawn, patrolling the kitchen, finding new ways to nag. Settling that with a treat effectively voids the day's arithmetic. Redirecting to another form of attention — play, brushing, a window seat — works better, because food is not always what the cat is asking for. And everyone in the household needs to know the rules. The most common reason a cat diet fails is not the food; it is the one person quietly handing out treats.

Can I just cut the food right back and get it over with quickly?

With cats this is the single most dangerous approach. A cat tolerates going without food far less well than a dog or a person. When body fat is mobilised all at once it floods the liver, fat accumulates inside the liver cells and they stop working — this is hepatic lipidosis, or fatty liver. VCA states that it can begin after three to four consecutive days of eating little or nothing, and that the risk is much higher in a cat that was overweight to begin with. Treatment usually means a feeding tube placed into the oesophagus or stomach, with an average of six to seven weeks of tube feeding before the cat eats normally again. Weight loss should stay within 0.5 to 2% of body weight per week, and calories should be reduced in steps rather than in one cut.

How do I tell at home whether my cat is overweight?

Feeling the body tells you more than the number on the scale. The body condition score used in practice runs from 1 to 9, and 4.5 to 5 is ideal. Check three things. First, run a flat hand along the ribcage: the ribs should feel roughly like the bones on the back of your hand. If you have to press to find them, the cat is carrying extra weight. Second, look down from above — there should be a slight waist behind the ribs. Third, look from the side: the line of the belly should rise from the chest toward the hind legs. The loose flap of skin low on the belly, the primordial pouch, is normal anatomy and is not evidence of obesity. Cornell's Feline Health Center defines obesity as being 20% or more above normal body weight.

My cat gained weight after being neutered. Is that expected?

It is very common. After neutering, sex hormones fall and resting energy needs drop while appetite tends to rise, so the same portion that used to maintain weight now adds it. The Purina Institute recommends reducing caloric intake by around 30% after spaying or neutering. That figure is an average, so apply it and then weigh every two to four weeks and adjust. The important point is that adjusting portions from the day of surgery is far easier than taking weight off later. If the cat has been free-fed, this is the moment to switch to measured meals.

I have several cats. Can I put just one of them on a diet?

You can, but separate bowls are not enough. Cats will happily finish another cat's food, so the feeding itself has to be separated. Feeding each cat in its own room and removing the bowls once they have finished is the most reliable method. Other options are placing the lean cat's food somewhere the heavier cat cannot easily reach, such as high on a cat tree or inside a narrow box, or using a feeder that reads a microchip and only opens for one cat. Trying to slim one cat down while free-feeding the household is, realistically, not going to work.
