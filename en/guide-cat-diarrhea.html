Cat Diarrhea — How Long Can You Wait?

The bottom line first: if your cat is eating and playing normally you can watch for a day or two, but vomiting alongside it, or blood in the stool, means going in the same day.

Diarrhea is a signal, not a diagnosis

When you find a puddle instead of a formed stool in the litter box, the instinct is to hunt for one cause — usually something the cat ate. But diarrhea is not a disease in itself. It is the result of the gut failing to absorb water properly, and the reason behind it might be yesterday's new treat, a parasite that has been quietly building for months, or a problem in an entirely different organ such as the thyroid or the kidneys. Which is why sorting out what kind of diarrhea this is matters more than looking for something that is "good for diarrhea".

It helps to start with a definition. Cornell University's Feline Health Center describes feline diarrhea as "abnormally frequent passage of watery, sometimes oddly coloured (grey or yellow), and uncharacteristically foul smelling stools." The word doing the work there is frequent. A cat that used to go once a day and still goes once a day, just softer, is in a less pressing situation than a cat whose trips to the box have obviously multiplied.

By duration, there are two categories. PetMD puts acute diarrhea at under 14 days and chronic diarrhea at two to three weeks or longer. Acute cases lean toward simpler explanations — a diet change, stress, an infection. Chronic cases shift the weight toward inflammatory bowel disease, stubborn parasites, poorly controlled metabolic disease and cancer, none of which you can identify from the litter box. So "it has been three weeks and it comes and goes" is not a mild version of the problem; it is chronic diarrhea, and it is past the point of home management.

One practical request before anything else: photograph the stool. Once the box is cleaned, you are left answering questions about colour, consistency, mucus and blood from memory. A single photo settles half of the assessment below. Better still, bring a sample in a sealed container — fecal testing needs actual stool, and fresher samples give better results.

Small bowel or large bowel?

This is the first split a veterinarian makes, because the location of the problem changes both the list of suspects and the order of tests. The Merck Veterinary Manual sets the distinguishing features out like this.

What you observeSmall intestinalLarge intestinal
Frequency of defecationNormal or slightly increasedVery frequent
Volume per attemptNormal to increasedDecreased
UrgencyAbsentUsually present
Straining (tenesmus)AbsentUsually present
Mucus in the stoolUsually absentFrequent
Blood, when presentDark black (melena)Red and fresh
Weight lossMay be presentRare

In everyday terms: one trip to the box producing a large loose stool, in a cat that is losing weight, points to the small intestine. Repeated trips every ten minutes producing very little, with straining, jelly-like mucus or streaks of fresh red blood, points to the colon. Large bowel diarrhea looks more alarming because you can see the blood, while small bowel diarrhea progresses quietly — yet the small bowel version is often the one doing more long-term damage, because that is where nutrients are absorbed. When it fails, weight and muscle go with it.

A streak of fresh red blood is not automatically an emergency; an irritated colon lining can bleed a little at the surface. Black, tarry stool is a different matter. That colour comes from blood that has been digested on its way down from the stomach or upper small intestine, which raises the question of an ulcer or upper GI bleeding. Colour alone is enough reason to go in.

Common causes, from the food bowl to the thyroid

Different sources group the causes differently, but in the order they tend to turn up in practice, the list looks roughly like this.

  • A sudden change of diet — new food swapped in overnight, a new treat, a first tin of wet food. Hill's makes the same point, that abrupt food changes and new treats can temporarily disturb the gut. The most common cause and usually the fastest to resolve.
  • Stress — moving house, visitors, a new pet, a vet visit, a relocated litter box. Cats route environmental change through the gut.
  • Parasites — roundworms, tapeworms, Giardia, coccidia. Especially common in kittens and in recently rescued or adopted cats, and often present in cats that look perfectly well.
  • Tritrichomonas foetus — less talked about but an important cause of chronic large bowel diarrhea, with a distinctive picture. Extremely foul-smelling diarrhea with mucus and fresh red blood, in a cat with a normal appetite that is not vomiting and not losing weight. It clusters in multi-cat households, catteries and shelters and in young cats, and PCR is the most sensitive test. Left untreated, spontaneous resolution can take two years or longer, and cats often remain carriers.
  • Viral and bacterial infection — feline panleukopenia causes severe diarrhea, vomiting and rapid dehydration, and is an emergency. It is one of the core vaccinations, so it is worth checking that your cat's schedule has not slipped using the cat vaccination schedule calculator.
  • Underlying disease — chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, pancreatitis, liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease, low-grade small cell lymphoma. In particular, a middle-aged or older cat that is eating well but losing weight with diarrhea puts hyperthyroidism and intestinal lymphoma on the list together.
  • Medications — loose stool during a course of antibiotics is common. Tell the prescribing clinic rather than stopping on your own.
  • Something it should not have eaten — milk, human food, spoiled food, toxic plants. In hot weather, wet food left out too long is a frequent culprit. If something specific comes to mind, check it first with the cat toxic food checker.

That last item spikes in summer. Opened wet food spoils quickly at room temperature, and households used to free feeding often leave a morning portion out until evening — enough on its own to cause acute diarrhea when it is warm. Dry food is not exempt either; heat and humidity turn fats rancid, so large bags belong sealed and somewhere cool.

It is also common for more than one cause to be stacked. If a cat was stressed by a move, had its food changed in the same week, and happened to be carrying parasites, fixing only one of the three will not bring the stool back. That is precisely why, when a few days of adjustment at home change nothing, testing beats guessing.

Reading colour and consistency

Colour alone cannot diagnose anything, but it points you in a direction. Grouping what PetMD describes, the patterns you are most likely to see are these.

These are directions, not conclusions. An accurate diagnosis has to come from a veterinary clinic. The same yellow stool ranks differently in a two-year-old cat than in a fourteen-year-old, and blood that appears once is a different problem from blood in the second week.

When to wait, and when not to

This is the most common question and also the point where published guidance disagrees most. Cornell's Feline Health Center advises prompt care if diarrhea persists beyond one to two days, especially with poor appetite, lethargy or vomiting. PetMD draws a tighter line — 24 hours for kittens, senior cats, chronically ill cats and pregnant cats — and states that vomiting together with diarrhea is always an emergency. Small Door Veterinary says go immediately for loss of appetite, concurrent vomiting or bloody stool, and to contact the clinic within 24 to 48 hours if diarrhea persists or worsens otherwise. Knowing that the thresholds differ is itself useful.

SituationWhat it calls for
Diarrhea with repeated vomitingGo now — dehydration moves fast
Lethargy, hiding, unwilling to come outGo now
Black tarry stool, or heavy fresh bloodGo now
Watery stool with an extreme smellGo now
Kitten under 6 months, senior cat, or existing illnessDo not let it pass 24 hours

Dry gums, skin slow to fall back when pinchedDehydration — same day
Eating, playing, loose stool only, no blood24–48 hours of watching is reasonable
Loose stool on and off for 2–3 weeksChronic — this is a testing stage, not a watching one

You can check hydration roughly at home. If you lift a fold of skin at the scruff and it does not snap straight back, fluid has been lost. Gums that feel tacky or dry rather than slick point the same way. Bear in mind that older cats have less skin elasticity to begin with, so this test alone is not conclusive in a senior.

At the clinic the sequence is usually fecal testing (parasites, PCR) → bloodwork (complete blood count and serum chemistry) → imaging if needed (radiographs or ultrasound), with endoscopy and biopsy reserved for chronic or unexplained cases. Bringing a written note of when it started, how many times a day, the colour and consistency, and what has happened to appetite and weight can shorten that sequence — which is why the photo and the sample are worth the trouble.

What helps at home, and what you must not do

Start with the must-nots, because these are the two things that most often make a cat worse.

  • Do not withhold food on your own judgement. Brief fasting is sometimes mentioned for dogs, but applying that to a cat is dangerous. A cat that goes a few days without eating can develop hepatic lipidosis, or fatty liver, and the University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine notes that overweight and obese cats are at greater risk. Their advice is unambiguous: if a cat stops eating, seek veterinary attention promptly rather than waiting it out. Whether to withhold food is a decision for your vet.
  • Do not give human anti-diarrheal medication. PetMD says so explicitly. Pepto-Bismol and Kaopectate contain salicylate, from the same family as aspirin, and cats metabolise it poorly. Loperamide (Imodium) is likewise not something to give on your own. When the cause is an infection or a foreign object, forcing the bowel to stop moving traps the problem inside.

What you can do, when the case is mild and the cat is otherwise bright:

  • Keep water available everywhere. Replacing what is being lost matters most. Put bowls in several rooms, and mixing in wet food helps. For how much your cat should be drinking by weight, see the pet water intake calculator.
  • Keep the usual food, but split the portions. Do not add new treats or a new food right now. Smaller, more frequent meals sit easier on an irritated gut. Check the daily amount with the cat food portion calculator and divide it into three or four servings.
  • Cat-specific probiotics, starting small. Use a product formulated for cats rather than a human one, and begin with a small amount while watching the response.
  • Keep the litter box spotless. Cats hold it in when the box is unpleasant, which is the last thing you want during diarrhea. Scoop often and keep one more box than you have cats.
  • Transition food over 7 to 10 days. If a diet change triggered this episode, next time mix the new food in and raise the proportion gradually. The same applies when going back to the old food.
  • Write it down. Date, frequency, colour, consistency, how much was eaten, body weight. In chronic cases this record can be as informative as the test results.

The point worth ending on is that cats hide illness well. Diarrhea happens quietly in a box, and it can take days for anyone to notice, which is why so many owners arrive at the clinic unable to say when it started — and why the cat is often further along than expected. If the stool is not what it usually is, booking an appointment beats reading more. The diarrhea itself is rarely the real problem; the cause behind it is, and that is not something you can identify at home.

Vomiting alongside diarrhea, and the opposite problem of a cat that cannot pass stool at all, each need a different approach. For which foods are safe and which are not, the complete guide to foods dogs and cats can't eat covers them in one place.

How many days can I watch my cat's diarrhea at home?

If your cat is an adult, still eating and playing normally, and there is no blood in the stool, 24 to 48 hours of watching is reasonable. Sources differ on the exact line. Cornell's Feline Health Center advises prompt veterinary care if diarrhea lasts more than a day or two, especially alongside poor appetite, lethargy or vomiting. PetMD is stricter and says not to exceed 24 hours in kittens, senior cats, cats with existing illness, or pregnant cats. If vomiting appears alongside the diarrhea, go in immediately regardless of how long it has been.

Should I fast my cat for a day when it has diarrhea?

Do not fast a cat on your own judgement. A cat that goes without eating for a few days in a row can develop hepatic lipidosis, or fatty liver, and the University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine notes that overweight and obese cats are at greater risk. Their advice is to seek veterinary attention promptly rather than let a cat go without food. Keep water and food accessible, and treat a cat that will not eat on its own as a reason to go in, not a reason to wait.

Can I give a small dose of a human anti-diarrheal like Pepto-Bismol or Imodium?

No. Pepto-Bismol and Kaopectate contain salicylate, which is in the same family as aspirin, and cats metabolise it poorly. Loperamide, sold as Imodium, is also not something to give on your own. If the underlying cause is an infection or a foreign object, forcing the gut to stop moving can make things worse rather than better. PetMD states plainly that human over-the-counter anti-diarrheal medications should never be used. Any medication should come from your veterinarian.

The diarrhea started after I changed food. Should I switch straight back?

Switching back abruptly unsettles the gut a second time. Return to the old food, but shift the ratio over several days rather than in one meal, and when you try a new food again, move across 7 to 10 days. Keep in mind that a diet change coinciding with the diarrhea does not prove it was the cause. If the loose stool continues after you have gone back, a fecal parasite test is the logical next step.
