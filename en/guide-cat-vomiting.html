Cat Vomiting — Hairball or Something Else?

The bottom line first: what matters is not that your cat threw up once, but how often. Once every two weeks is widely treated as unremarkable, but past one episode a week, stop filing it under hairballs and get it checked.

"She's always been a pukey cat" is the most dangerous sentence

If you live with a cat you know the sound. A few hacking noises somewhere in the dark, and in the morning there is a long damp cylinder in the middle of the living room floor. Most owners file it under hairballs and move on, because everyone knows cats throw up.

This is exactly where veterinary sources diverge. Cornell's Feline Health Center says "it is not uncommon for a cat to expel a hairball once every week or two without any enduring problems," and in the same document classifies vomiting more often than once a week as frequent. Meanwhile there is clinical material written by practising vets titled, bluntly, "Hairballs Are Not Normal," which argues that cats vomiting more often than every two weeks are significantly more likely to have underlying gastrointestinal disease.

So the line for "normal" moves depending on who is writing. The direction of travel is consistent, though — the older habit of writing off frequent feline vomiting as a personality trait is giving way to treating it as a symptom. This article follows that view, but rather than simply alarming you, it tries to be specific about where the conversation stops being about hairballs.

Pay particular attention to that last row, because it is the one owners miss. Even if the number of episodes has not increased, a body weight that is quietly drifting downward is no longer a hairball story. A cat's coat hides a great deal, so weight change is very hard to see by eye. That is why weighing once a month on the same scale, and writing the number down, is worth the trouble.

Vomiting, regurgitation and coughing are three different things

This is also the first thing your vet will try to establish, because whether the episode you described was actually vomiting completely changes where the workup starts. Here is how the three differ.

  • Vomiting — there is a build-up. The cat swallows repeatedly, the abdomen contracts in waves, and there are several dry heaves before anything appears. Because the material comes from the stomach or intestine, it is partly digested and often mixed with yellow bile.
  • Regurgitation — there is no build-up. Shortly after eating, the cat lowers its head and the food simply falls out. The contents are usually intact kibble, sometimes in a tube shape moulded by the oesophagus. This points at the oesophagus rather than the stomach, so the diagnostic route is different.
  • Coughing and asthma — the most confusing of the three. A cat with feline asthma crouches low with the neck stretched forward and hacks, which looks almost exactly like a cat working up a hairball. The difference is that nothing comes out. Multiple sources repeatedly warn about cases that turned out to be asthma or heartworm rather than hairballs.

If you cannot tell them apart, there is one reliable answer: film it. These episodes almost never repeat in the exam room, so a ten-second video is worth more than ten sentences of description. Note when the cat last ate and how many minutes elapsed, too.

One practical note: if your cat throws up soon after meals, suspect eating speed first. A deep narrow bowl piled with kibble encourages gulping and swallowing air along with it. Switching to a wide shallow dish and splitting the day's ration into three or four meals eliminates more vomiting than you would expect. Reading the colour and contents

What the vomit looks like will not give you a diagnosis — the same yellow liquid can mean an empty stomach or a pancreatic problem. But it is enough to decide whether to call the clinic now or watch until morning. Here are the forms you are most likely to see.

Green vomit with a strong foul smell is the combination to take most seriously. It can mean material from lower down the intestine has travelled backwards, which is a pattern seen with obstruction. If it comes together with a distended, painful abdomen and a very flat, lethargic cat, look for an emergency clinic even in the middle of the night.

At the other end, yellow bile is more common than people think. If the pattern is early-morning yellow liquid after twelve or more hours without food, adding a small late-night meal sometimes makes it disappear entirely. But if you try that for several days and nothing changes, the empty stomach was not the reason — get it checked.

And before you clean it up, take one photograph. Saying "it was yellow" and showing a picture carry completely different amounts of information. Some owners scoop the material onto a tissue and bring it in, which is genuinely more useful when foreign material may be mixed in.

Do not wait on these — the red flags

The following are not "maybe we'll go in the morning" situations. These are the ones sources consistently say need immediate attention.

  • Any sign your cat swallowed string or thread — the most dangerous form of foreign body in cats. If one end anchors under the tongue or at the anus while the rest travels into the intestine, the bowel can bunch up along the string like an accordion and the intestinal wall can be cut through. This is why you must never pull on a string you can see. If there is thread visible at the mouth or the rear, leave it alone and go straight to the clinic.
  • Repeated retching that produces nothing — can signal an obstruction or a serious gastric problem.
  • Vomiting plus diarrhoea together — dehydration develops quickly, and it is most dangerous in kittens and senior cats.
  • Blood, either fresh red or coffee-ground
  • Lethargy, hiding, refusing both food and water — cats hide when they feel ill. A cat who is normally out in the open and is suddenly wedged in a corner is telling you something.
  • Drinking much more, or urinating noticeably more or less — that combination appears in kidney disease, diabetes and thyroid problems.
  • Vomiting after chewing a plant, especially a lily — lilies can cause acute kidney failure in cats, and even the pollen is treated as dangerous. Do not wait for symptoms; call the clinic.
  • Vomiting after eating a suspect food or medication — bring the packaging so the vet knows what and how much. To check a specific food quickly, type the name into the cat toxic food checker.

One more thing belongs here. Not eating anything for more than 24 hours is an independent warning sign in cats. If an overweight cat goes several days without food, fat can accumulate in the liver — hepatic lipidosis — so "let's wait until the appetite comes back" can create a second illness. The fasting people routinely do with dogs should not be copied across to cats.

What the workup usually looks like

Knowing the sequence in advance makes the visit much less stressful. Published veterinary material describes an approach to chronic vomiting along roughly these lines.

  • Step 1 — history and physical exam. When it started, how often, what came out, whether the weight has changed. The vet palpates the neck to check the thyroid and the abdomen for masses or pain. Your records are what set the pace here.
  • Step 2 — basic bloodwork and a faecal test. Kidney values, liver values, blood glucose, and in an older cat thyroid hormone (T4). Parasites are checked at this stage too.
  • Step 3 — imaging. If steps 1 and 2 do not answer it, radiographs and ultrasound look for foreign material, intestinal wall thickening and masses. Linear foreign bodies such as thread often do not show up directly, so bunched intestinal loops and gas patterns are read as indirect evidence.
  • Step 4 — gastrointestinal profile. Cobalamin (vitamin B12), folate and feline-specific pancreatic lipase. Cobalamin can fall in chronic gastrointestinal disease, and supplementation is sometimes given alongside treatment when it is low.
  • Step 5 — endoscopy or biopsy. Chronic enteropathy and gastrointestinal lymphoma cannot be told apart on signs alone, so a tissue sample is sometimes required for the final distinction.

Not every cat goes all the way to step 5. Most find their direction at step 1 or 2, and only move on when needed. What is worth avoiding is the situation where "try a hairball remedy and come back if it doesn't help" quietly consumes several months. If frequent vomiting has been going on for more than two months, it is entirely reasonable for you to raise the subject of testing first.

Diagnosis and prescription must come from a veterinarian. The thresholds in this article are general reference points to help you decide when to act; the same signs can lead to completely different conclusions depending on age and existing conditions.

What you can change at home

Once underlying disease has been ruled out, these are the things that actually reduce the number of episodes.

  • Brush more. The most direct way to cut how much hair gets swallowed. Two or three times a week for short coats, daily for long coats, and more often during a heavy shed. One brush rarely does everything — an undercoat tool plus a finishing comb works better.
  • Increase water intake. When a cat is short on water, swallowed hair moves through to the stool less easily. Multiple bowls in different rooms, placed away from the food, plus some wet food, all help. To see roughly how much your cat should drink for its weight, use the pet water intake calculator.
  • Slow the eating down. Wide shallow dishes, smaller portions more often, and separate feeding stations in a multi-cat home. Competitive eating can be the whole cause.
  • Transition food slowly. Swapping to a new diet all at once can cause vomiting on its own. Mix it into the old food over about a week, raising the proportion gradually.
  • Remove anything swallowable. Thread, rubber bands, wand-toy strings, plastic film, hair ties, mask loops. In a house with a cat, none of these belong on the floor. Wand toys in particular should go into a closed drawer the moment play ends.
  • Keep a log. Date, time, how long after a meal, what came out. One line in a calendar app is enough, and it will be the most useful thing you bring to the next appointment.

My cat brings up a hairball two or three times a month. Is that normal?

It is hard to call that flatly normal, but it is a good point to start keeping records. Cornell's Feline Health Center describes bringing up a hairball once every week or two as something many cats do without lasting problems, while several clinical sources recommend a workup once a cat exceeds one episode a week. Two or three times a month sits in between, which is exactly why it feels ambiguous. Do not look at frequency alone, though. Watch the weight. If the number of episodes has not changed but the body weight is slowly dropping, that is no longer a hairball question. Mark the dates on a calendar and weigh your cat once a month on the same scale, and your vet will reach a conclusion far faster.

My cat threw up right after eating and the kibble came back up undigested.

If undigested kibble comes back up shortly after a meal, sometimes in a tube-like shape, that may be regurgitation from the oesophagus rather than true vomiting. Regurgitation happens suddenly with no abdominal effort, while vomiting is preceded by wave-like contractions of the belly and several dry heaves. The two involve different organs, so they send the vet down different diagnostic paths. When a cat eats too fast and brings the meal straight back, switching to a wide shallow dish and splitting the daily ration into several smaller meals often solves it. If it continues anyway, the oesophagus may be involved and it is worth an exam. A short phone video of the episode helps a great deal.

My cat retches but nothing comes out.

There are two very different possibilities. One is that the cat really is trying to vomit and cannot. The other is that this is not vomiting at all but coughing. Feline asthma makes a cat crouch low with the neck stretched forward and hack, which looks almost identical to a cat working up a hairball. The difference is that nothing comes out. Cats are not rarely diagnosed with asthma after months of the owner assuming hairballs. Watch the posture, and if the dry hacking repeats with nothing produced, film it and show your vet. If your cat retches repeatedly with nothing produced and the belly looks distended or the cat becomes very lethargic, suspect an obstruction and go in immediately.

Should I withhold food after my cat vomits? What about water?

Cats are not dogs here, and prolonged fasting on your own initiative is not safe. In particular, an overweight cat that goes several days without eating can develop hepatic lipidosis, where fat accumulates in the liver, so withholding food can create a new disease. If your cat vomited once and is otherwise bright and normal, offering the next meal at about half the usual size and split into small portions is reasonable, and water should stay available rather than being taken away. If the cat eats nothing for more than about twelve hours, or the vomiting repeats, that is past the point of home adjustment and belongs at the clinic. Do not give human stomach medication on your own.
